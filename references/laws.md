# Laws of UX that matter in a working product

Use these to explain a finding and to choose between two fixes. They are not a checklist to recite. Source: lawsofux.com by Jon Yablonski, and the research behind each law.

- **Hick's law.** The more options, the longer the decision. Reduce the choices at each step, set a good default, move rare options out of the main path. Applies to: radio groups, menus, toolbars, settings pages.
- **Fitts's law.** Big and near targets are fast; small and far ones are slow and error-prone. Put the main action where the hand already is, make frequent targets large, keep destructive actions away from frequent ones.
- **Jakob's law.** People spend most of their time in other products and expect yours to work the same way. Use the standard pattern and the pattern Rule already uses elsewhere before inventing one.
- **Miller's law and chunking.** People hold only a few things in mind. Group related fields and options, show what a decision depends on, never make people carry a value from one screen to another.
- **Doherty threshold.** Responses under about 400 milliseconds feel instant. Anything slower needs immediate feedback: a pressed state, a loader on the control, a skeleton.
- **Tesler's law.** Every task has complexity that cannot be removed, only moved. Move it to the system: compute, default, remember, prefill, instead of asking the person.
- **Postel's law.** Accept input in whatever reasonable form people give it (spaces in numbers, pasted lists, any date format), and be strict in what you show back.
- **Peak-end rule.** People judge a flow by its worst moment and its end. Fix the most painful step first and make the final confirmation clear.
- **Goal-gradient.** People speed up as they see the end. In multi-step flows show how many steps there are and where the person is.
- **Von Restorff effect.** The thing that differs is remembered. One primary action per view; if everything is emphasized, nothing is.
- **Zeigarnik effect.** Unfinished tasks stay in mind. Show drafts and incomplete setups clearly so people can come back and finish.
